Teaching

A method, a measurement, and a limit

Students should leave able to choose a method, report uncertainty, and say when a model should not be used.

I teach quantitative methods by connecting them to a measurement students can defend. Lectures state the principle; a dataset, a missing label, or a physical constraint makes the principle necessary. Students should be able to pose a question, choose a method, report uncertainty, and say when a model should not be used.

Preparation in programming, statistics, and domain science is uneven, so I start from shared foundations and increase independence: worked examples, structured labs, then open projects. Reproducibility, calibration, and subgroup performance are part of the work, not the last slide.

I judge teaching by whether students can choose a method, report uncertainty, and explain limits — not only by exam scores. Mixed-background rooms need scaffolds, not lower standards.

Courses I am prepared to teach

Statistical learning and machine learning

Core methods with an emphasis on evaluation: cross-validation, calibration, ablation, and when not to trust a score.

Data science for incomplete observations

Missing modalities, weak labels, irregular time, and subgroup performance — the usual state of biological data.

Imaging and signals as physical measurements

OCT, spectroscopy, and functional tests treated as the output of an optical or physiological process, not as generic tensors.

Evaluation of AI models

Bias, failure modes, uncertainty, and reporting that a clinician or a scientist can defend.

Statistical learning for incomplete biological data

A lab-linked course. Students move from problem definition through validation and communication, using vision data or collaborator datasets from statistics, brain sciences, physics, or biology.

Mentoring

I have mentored more than twenty undergraduate and high-school researchers on imaging and predictive modeling, at Wellesley, Wichita State, and UMass Amherst, and I co-teach with clinical and computational colleagues at Harvard.

In the lab I use written expectations, milestones, and a shift from close supervision to independent aims. Trainees learn both the algorithm and the measurement: why a label fails, what an optical system actually records, and how to report uncertainty.
